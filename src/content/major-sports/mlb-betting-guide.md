Baseball (MLB) has the most distinctive odds structure. Unlike basketball or soccer, it does not lead with the point spread; you look first at the "moneyline," then at the "run line." The first thing a beginner should do is avoid mixing the two up.

## 1. Moneyline vs the run line

- **Moneyline**: betting on who wins. Baseball scores are low, so a favorite's win rate is not as lopsided as in basketball, which is why the moneyline is the mainstream play.
- **The run line (<details class="term"><summary>run line</summary><span class="term-note">Baseball's name for the point spread, almost always set at 1.5 runs. Because baseball scores are low, 1.5 runs is already a large margin.</span></details>, usually 1.5 runs)**: the favorite gives the underdog 1.5 runs. Because baseball is low-scoring, 1.5 runs is already a large margin — the favorite must win by 2 runs for you to win.

Start with the moneyline, and once you can read how the odds reflect strength, then touch the run line.

## 2. The key variable: the starting pitcher

Baseball has a variable no other team sport has: the <details class="term"><summary>starting pitcher</summary><span class="term-note">The pitcher who opens the game and throws most of it. His form directly decides the game; an ace starter on the mound visibly lifts the whole team's win rate.</span></details>. In a game, the starting pitcher throws most of the innings, and his form directly decides how the game unfolds.

- An ace starter on the mound visibly lifts the whole team's win rate.
- When the starting rotation changes or his form slips, the line moves immediately.

**Before you bet, check who is starting today.** This is the single best-value step in baseball betting, far more useful than looking at team records.

## 3. A long season: big sample, big single-game swings

MLB plays 162 games a season, one of the largest samples in professional sports. That cuts two ways:

- The good side: a player's or team's true quality gets averaged out over many games — "the numbers don't lie."
- The bad side: single games still swing hard — one starter blowing up or one error lets a weak team steal a game.

So do not assume a long season makes a single game predictable. Length only means the long-term patterns are reliable, not that tonight's game is easy to call.

## 4. Common mistakes

- Mixing up the moneyline and the run line, and miscounting your payout.
- Looking only at team records without checking the starting pitcher.
- Assuming "favorite beats underdog" and underestimating baseball's single-game swings.

## What this page should leave you with

1. First separate the moneyline from the run line (which is usually 1.5 runs).
2. The starting pitcher is baseball's most unique variable — always check before you bet.
3. A long season does not make a single game easy to call — do not get drunk on "transparent data."
4. Only stake money you can afford to lose — baseball's single games swing harder than you think.
