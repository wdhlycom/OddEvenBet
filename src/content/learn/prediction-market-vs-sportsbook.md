They look similar — you put money down on an outcome and get paid if you are right. But underneath, they are different machines, and the difference determines everything from how prices move to how you are treated when you win.

## The counterparty

**Sportsbook**: you bet *against the book*. The operator sets the odds, takes the other side, and builds its margin into the price. If you win, the book pays you; if you lose, the book keeps it.

**Prediction market**: you trade *against another person*. There is no house. Someone sold you the contract, and the platform only matches the two of you and runs settlement.

This one difference cascades into everything else.

## How the price is set

**Sportsbook**: a pricing team sets odds, then adjusts them to balance their book and protect their margin. The odds you see are a *price offered to you*, with the margin already hidden inside.

**Prediction market**: the price is whatever the last trade agreed on, set by supply and demand on an open order book. There is no margin hidden in the price — instead, costs are *explicit*, as a fee and a spread you can see.

## Can you exit early?

**Sportsbook**: generally no. Once you place a bet, you are locked in until the event settles (cash-out offers exist but are priced by the book).

**Prediction market**: yes. You can sell your contract at any time before resolution. This is why prediction market prices react to news within minutes — anyone can trade out of a position the moment information changes.

## How the venue treats winners

**Sportsbook**: a book can limit your stakes or close your account if you win consistently, because you are costing them money.

**Prediction market**: the platform does not lose when you win — your counterparty does. So there is no incentive for the venue to limit or ban profitable traders. (The risk shifts to the opposite problem: you may be trading against people with better information than you.)

## Regulation

**Sportsbook**: a mature, licensed industry. In regulated markets you have a named regulator, deposit protection, and a dispute process.

**Prediction market**: a newer, patchier picture. Some venues are licensed and regulated; crypto-native ones often are not, which means your recourse when something goes wrong is much thinner.

## The practical summary

| | Sportsbook | Prediction market |
|---|---|---|
| Counterparty | The book | Another user |
| Price | Set by book, margin hidden | Set by order book, costs explicit |
| Exit early | Rarely | Yes, any time |
| Winner treatment | Can be limited/banned | No venue incentive to ban |
| Recourse | Regulator | Varies, often limited |

Neither is inherently better. If you want a regulated, simple product and accept the hidden margin, a licensed sportsbook is the cleaner choice. If you want transparent pricing, early exit, and no house limiting you — and you accept self-custody and thinner recourse — a prediction market is the fit.
