In sports betting there's one product the books promote hardest, with ads everywhere, and everyone thinks it's "a small stake for a big prize" — the <details class="term"><summary>parlay</summary><span class="term-note">parlay / accumulator — bundling two or more bets into a single ticket where every leg must win to pay out, and the odds multiply. Also called an accumulator or multi.</span></details>. This article lays its math out for you, and once you see it, you'll understand exactly why the book loves it to death.

## What a parlay is

Several bets wrapped into one ticket. Say three football games, one result each, bundled into a single parlay ticket:

- **Every leg must win** for the ticket to pay, with each leg's odds multiplied together;
- **Any leg loses** and the whole ticket is void — you get nothing back.

Three ordinary -110 bets are nothing special individually. Bundled into a parlay, the multiplied odds come to about +596 — a $10 stake returns $69. That number is the parlay's entire appeal.

## Why the odds are inflated

That tempting big number hides a truth: **every leg has already had vig taken out of it, and when you parlay, that vig multiplies.**

Three genuinely 50/50 games have a fair parlay price of about +700. But the book only gives you +596. That missing slice is the <details class="term"><summary>house edge</summary><span class="term-note">the percentage the book takes from you in the long run. A single bet is roughly 4–5%; a parlay multiplies that edge, and more legs means a bigger edge.</span></details> — collected three times over.

More painfully, **the win rate collapses fast**:

- One -110 leg: about 52% win rate;
- Two legs: about 27%;
- Three legs: about 14%;
- Four legs: about 7%;
- Five legs: about 3.9%.

**By five legs, you're winning fewer than 4% of tickets, while the book is only paying you at 4% probability.** That's what happens when you multiply the take several times over — which is exactly why the book pushes parlays so hard: it's the highest-margin product in the shop.

## The one theoretical exception

To be fair, a parlay shouldn't always be written off entirely. There's one narrow exception: **when you actually have an edge.**

If you can win 55% of -110 bets over the long run (something most people can't do), then a two-leg parlay actually returns better than single bets. But there's a deeper catch: **parlays are wildly volatile**, which forces you to bet even smaller under the Kelly formula — so that slightly higher return gets eaten away again by the smaller bet size.

So for the vast majority of people, the conclusion is simple: **parlays are fine as entertainment, but not as a strategy.**

## The only sensible way to use a parlay

Treat it as "paying for fun," not as an investment:

1. **Keep it to 2–3 legs.** Beyond that, you've entered lottery territory.
2. **Bet small.** Use the money you were going to spend on entertainment anyway — the money it's fine to lose.
3. **Don't chase.** The worst reaction is "so close, one leg off — let me do a bigger one to win it back." "One leg off" is a feeling, not progress.

## What this page should leave you with

1. A parlay is several bets bundled into one ticket that only pays if all of them hit; the multiplied odds are the temptation.
2. Every leg carries vig, and parlaying multiplies it — which is why it's the highest-margin product for the book.
3. Win rate collapses as legs pile up: five legs leaves you under a 4% win rate.
4. Unless you can win 55% long-term (very few can), a parlay is entertainment, not strategy.
5. Only stake money you can afford to lose.
