A prediction market has no bookmaker. No one sets your odds, and no one stands against you by default. It's an <details class="term"><summary>order book</summary><span class="term-note">The list of quotes posted by everyone who wants to buy or sell. The price you see is matched out of that list.</span></details>: you buy a contract from someone who wants to sell, and the price you agree on is the result.

## The contract is simple

Each market issues only two complementary contracts:

- **Yes**: pays the person who's right $1; wrong, $0.
- **No**: pays the person who's right $1; wrong, $0.

Exactly one of the two settles at $1. That's the entire game rule.

## The price is the probability

If a "Yes" contract sells for $0.68, the market is saying: this thing is about 68% likely to happen. That price isn't the platform's forecast — it's the consensus formed by everyone who put real money down, voting with their wallets.

Which is why the media loves quoting prediction market prices — they aren't free opinions; behind every cent there's someone who loses money if it's wrong.

## Why the price moves before the outcome

Unlike a fixed-odds lottery ticket, you can sell your contract before settlement. Buy "Yes" at $0.40, and when new information pushes it to $0.70, sell for a $0.30 gain — the event never had to actually happen. Because you can exit at any time, the price reacts to news within minutes instead of waiting for the end.

## What it is not

- **Not risk-free arbitrage.** Spreads and fees eat small edges.
- **Not a poll.** The price reflects money, and money is distributed very unevenly.
- **Not a guarantee of accuracy.** Thin markets on niche questions have poor <details class="term"><summary>liquidity</summary><span class="term-note">The ability to transact quickly in a market. Poor liquidity means a few trades can shove the price around, and mispricing goes uncorrected.</span></details>, and prices can be wrong for weeks with no one correcting them.
