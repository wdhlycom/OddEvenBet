A prediction market isn't one single thing. It's more like a bazaar with stalls of every kind. To avoid getting lost, first learn: what exactly each market is asking you to bet on, and what settles it when you win.

Those two questions are the most useful two axes for sorting the categories.

## Politics: elections and the legislative process

This is the category that made prediction markets famous — and where the media most loves quoting prices.

- **What you bet on**: who wins an election, whether a bill passes, whether an official steps down.
- **What settles it**: the official certified result. Whoever takes office, whether the bill is formally signed — there's an authoritative source with the final say.

Because the settlement source is clear and neutral enough, these prices get quoted as a "real-money version of a poll." But remember: price reflects money, and money is distributed unevenly — it's a different thing from the actual voting tendency.

## Macroeconomics and data: rate hikes, CPI, GDP

This category bets on the specific numbers the government is going to publish.

- **Typical questions**: will the rate rise this month, which range will CPI land in, is quarterly GDP positive or negative.
- **What settles it**: official data published by the government or central bank, usually precise to the decimal — almost no room to argue.

This category is a natural fit for regulated markets, because "who has the final say" is crystal clear. The number drops, settlement happens, and there's little to dispute.

## Crypto prices

Betting whether a coin hits a given price by a given time, or whether it outperforms another coin.

- **The upside**: high short-term volatility, fast settlement, technical in nature. Prices are public and checkable at any moment, so settlement is clean.
- **The cost**: you're playing against people who stare at charts all day, and news moves at light speed. If you don't understand the technicals, don't join in.

## Sports

Football, basketball, and other major sports — high volume, and lots of people understand them.

- **The upside**: many markets, mature rules, almost anyone can read them.
- **The downside**: there's no easy money here. The people across the table may have studied one team for a decade. The more crowded a market, the fewer mispricings.

## Entertainment and pop culture

Who wins an award ceremony, whether a movie's box office crosses a number.

- **What settles it**: results announced by the organizer, or data from a box-office tracker.
- **The risk**: these markets are often thin, with poor liquidity, and one or two big players can drag the price around. Fun to watch, but don't treat it as an investment.

## Corporate events: earnings, M&A, regulatory approval

Betting how much a company earns this quarter, whether a merger gets approved, whether some clearance lands.

- **The profile**: niche, thinnest liquidity, and mispricings can linger for a long time — which is *not* a good thing.
- **The risk**: a wrong price that no one corrects for a long time means you're more likely to get filled at the wrong price, and the timing of the correction is uncertain. For beginners, this is the category with the highest trap density.

## What this page should leave you with

1. Different categories carry completely different liquidity and settlement risk — don't treat one market as a stand-in for all prediction markets.
2. Practice first in a field you already understand; forcing yourself into an unfamiliar arena is paying to lose.
3. The settlement rule matters more than the category — nail down "who defines the result" before you bet.
4. The more niche the category, the more likely the mispricing — but that doesn't mean it's easier to profit; it usually just means worse liquidity.
5. Only stake money you can afford to lose.
