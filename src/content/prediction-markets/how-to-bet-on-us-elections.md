Elections are prediction markets' busiest season — and the easiest time for beginners to lose money. The reason isn't the market; it's you: **of ten people betting an election, nine are betting "who I want to win" rather than "who's more likely to win."** This page lays out the right way to bet an election.

## 1. What election-market categories exist

An election isn't one market, it's a whole ecosystem. The common ones:

- **President wins**: who becomes president — the most mainstream, most liquid market.
- **Popular vote**: who gets more total national votes. Note this is different from "who becomes president" — the US uses the Electoral College, so the popular-vote winner can lose the election.
- **Swing states**: who wins a given key state, like Pennsylvania, Michigan or Georgia. This is where the information is richest, and where homework pays most.
- **Senate / House control**: which party controls each chamber.

Don't bet everything at once as a beginner. The presidential market is the most liquid but its price is already "right" — your information edge is nearly zero; swing-state markets are easier to turn research into a call.

## 2. How to read the price

A price of 0.65 means the market thinks the event is about 65% likely to happen. **That isn't a sure thing, it's "sixty-five percent confidence"** — flip it around, and there's a 35% chance you lose the whole stake.

The classic election trap: when a candidate leads and the price pushes to 0.80 or 0.90, beginners feel it's "locked in" and go big. But 0.90 means nine wins in ten — and the one loss can take out the whole account.

## 3. The biggest trap: yourself

Political affiliation is poison for judgement. Who you support and who you hate will make you:

- Read "I hope he wins" as "he will win", overrating your own side's chances.
- Cherry-pick polls that flatter your side and ignore the ones that don't.
- Explain the result away with "rigged" or "fraud" rather than admitting the call was wrong.

**Before betting an election, ask yourself: if it were reversed, could I still place this bet?** If the answer's no, you're betting your politics, not the probability.

## 4. Cross-check instead of guessing

Single sources are unreliable, especially in elections. A few cross-checks that work:

- **Swing states vs national**: when national polls are tight, swing-state trends carry more information, because the Electoral College counts states, not total votes.
- **Price vs polls**: if the prediction-market price diverges clearly from the latest polls, either the market knows something you don't, or sentiment has overshot. When the two don't line up, don't rush in.
- **Historical context**: a state's turnout, mail-vote share and counting speed affect "when the result can be called", which in turn affects settlement.

## 5. Resolution criteria — don't bet the wrong rule

The finest, easiest-to-trip detail in election markets is the resolution rule. The same "who wins" can resolve differently across platforms:

- Does it follow media (AP) calls, or official certification?
- If a swing state goes to a recount or litigation, is the market frozen, or settled early under some rule?
- How are candidate withdrawals or third-party spoilers handled?

**Before you order, read that market's resolution rule in full.** In a high-controversy event like an election, the difference in resolution criteria can be the exact thing that decides whether you win or lose.

## What this page should leave you with

1. Elections are a whole ecosystem — beyond the presidential market, swing-state markets suit homework better.
2. 0.65 is sixty-five percent confidence, not a sure thing; the higher the price, the bigger the cost of the one loss.
3. Split "who I want to win" from "who will win" — your politics is judgement's biggest enemy.
4. Cross-check with swing states, prices and polls; don't lean on a single source.
5. Read the full resolution rule before you order — election resolution criteria are the easiest place to trip.
