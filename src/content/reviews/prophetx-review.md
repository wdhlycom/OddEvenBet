## What ProphetX actually is

A sports exchange built around back and lay orders. On most platforms you can only buy a market; here you can also sell it, which lets you price an outcome yourself or hedge an existing position. The quote is determined by supply and demand, not by a house margin, which is the core appeal for anyone used to bookmaker pricing.

## Fees

There is no embedded vig, and losing direct trades are free. The cost sits on the win side: 2% of net profit on direct orders, with parlay trades carrying a taker fee. For a losing or break-even trader that is nothing; for a consistent winner, a 2% haircut on net profit is a real, ongoing drag that many competitors don't charge on top of the spread.

## Liquidity

The order book is the product, and it is also the risk. On popular US sports and marquee events depth is decent, but on secondary markets — including some of ProphetX's niche coverage like F1 — spreads widen and depth thins. You are supplying your own execution, so a bad fill is your cost to bear, not the platform's.

## Licensing and state risk

ProphetX is CFTC-regulated and runs US-dollar accounts, advertised across roughly 49 states plus Washington, DC. The reality is narrower: Ohio has already ordered sports contracts to stop, which is a concrete example that the state-level map is more fragile than the headline number suggests. Expect further state-by-state challenges.

## Who this is wrong for

If you want to set-and-forget a fixed price, or you trade in states that have pushed back, look elsewhere. If you are an advanced trader who wants to back and lay, hedge positions, and is comfortable paying 2% of winnings for the privilege, the architecture is genuinely differentiated.
